Lesson 0002 · Unit 1 — Expressions and Equations

Distributing a Negative

One sign, and every term inside the parentheses changes. This is where most of the lost points in Unit 1 actually come from.

In Lesson 0001 you learned to move variable terms across the equals sign. Now the parentheses show up — and with them, the single most common way to lose points in this entire unit.

Here's the thing: nobody gets these wrong because the idea is hard. They get them wrong because they go fast, and a minus sign is small. Your teacher has seen this mistake a thousand times. Getting immune to it is one of the cheapest ways to pull ahead of the class.

The invisible −1

When you see a minus sign sitting in front of parentheses with nothing else between them, there is a −1 hiding there:

−(x + 3)  really means  −1(x + 3)

Once you write that −1 in, this stops being a special case. It's just the distributive property you already know — multiply the outside by every term inside.

And because multiplying by −1 flips a sign, the result is: every term inside changes sign. Not just the first one. Every one.

Worked example 1 — the bare minus sign
−(x + 3)Start. There's a hidden −1 out front.
−1(x + 3)Write it in. Now it's ordinary distribution.
(−1)(x) + (−1)(3)Multiply the outside by each term inside.
−x − 3Both signs flipped. The +3 became −3.

Check it: pick x = 2. Original: −(2 + 3) = −5. Answer: −2 − 3 = −5. Same. ✓

The substitution trick — check any distribution in 10 seconds

Not sure you distributed right? Pick a number for x and try both versions. If the original and your answer give the same result, you almost certainly did it right. Use x = 2 — never use x = 0 or x = 1, because those hide sign errors.

This works on tests. It takes seconds and it catches exactly the mistake this lesson is about.

Worked example 2 — a negative times a negative
−4(2x − 3)Start.
(−4)(2x) + (−4)(−3)The term inside is −3 — carry its sign along.
−8x + 12Negative times negative is positive. That +12 is the whole point.

Check: at x = 2, original is −4(4 − 3) = −4; answer is −16 + 12 = −4. ✓

Worked example 3 — subtracting a whole parenthesis in an equation
10 − (3x − 4) = 20That minus applies to the entire parenthesis.
10 − 3x + 4 = 20Distribute the −1: −4 becomes +4.
14 − 3x = 20Combine the constants.
−3x = 6Subtract 14 from both sides.
x = −2Divide by −3.

Check: 10 − (3(−2) − 4) = 10 − (−10) = 20. ✓

The three ways this goes wrong

Every mistake on this topic is one of these three. Learn to recognize them and you can catch your own errors before you hand the paper in.

Error 1 — stopped early 
−3(x + 5)
= −3x + 5   ✗

The −3 only reached the x. It has to hit both terms.

Correct: −3x − 15

Error 2 — lost the flip 
−2(4x − 3)
= −8x − 6   ✗

Negative times negative is positive. (−2)(−3) = +6.

Correct: −8x + 6

Error 3 — missed the hidden −1 
5 − (2x − 3)
= 5 − 2x − 3   ✗

The minus belongs to the whole parenthesis, so the −3 flips to +3.

Correct: −2x + 8

Write it like the rubric is watching

  • Write the distribution step on its own line. Don't do it in your head. −3(x + 5) → −3x − 15 → then keep going. If you skip the line and get it wrong, you lose the whole problem instead of one step.
  • When a bare minus sits in front of parentheses, write the −1 in. It looks like extra work. It's the cheapest insurance in this unit.
  • Your syllabus grades justification — naming the distributive property when you use it is exactly the kind of precision that earns full credit.
