Lesson 0005 · Unit 1 — Expressions and Equations

Turning Words into Equations

The last piece of Unit 1 — and the part the syllabus grades hardest.

This is the last piece of Unit 1, and it's the one the syllabus cares about most: “model real-world situations using algebraic expressions.” Every unit test will have these, and they're where good algebra students lose marks — not because the algebra is hard, but because they translated the sentence wrong before they started.

Three steps, every single time:

  1. Name the unknown. Write "Let n = …" and say exactly what it is. This one line prevents most word-problem errors.
  2. Translate phrase by phrase, left to right, into symbols.
  3. Solve, then answer the actual question — which is often not the variable you solved for.

The translation table

Almost every word problem is built from these. The ones in the last column are where people get caught.

+ more than · sum · increased by · total · together
− less than · difference · decreased by · fewer than · take away
× times · twice (×2) · product · of · per · each
÷ quotient · per · split · shared equally
= is · equals · will be · results in · gives

“Less than” reverses the order. Always.

5 less than n is n − 5 — not 5 − n.

Think about what it means: you have n, and you take 5 away. Say it with numbers to feel it: “5 less than 20” is 15, and 20 − 5 = 15. If you wrote 5 − 20 you'd get −15, which is obviously wrong.

The same reversal hits “fewer than” and “subtracted from”. Everything else translates left to right; these don't.

Worked example 1 — translating a sentence

“Seven more than twice a number is 23. Find the number.”

Let n = the numberStep 1 — always name it first.
twice a number → 2n"Twice" means ×2.
seven more than that → 2n + 7"More than" adds — and adding is safe in either order.
2n + 7 = 23"Is" becomes the equals sign.
2n = 16Subtract 7.
n = 8Divide by 2. The number is 8.
Worked example 2 — a real situation

“A gym charges a $30 joining fee plus $15 per month. After how many months will you have paid $150?”

Let m = number of monthsName it, with units.
30 + 15m = 150$30 once, plus $15 for each of m months. "Per" signals multiplication.
15m = 120Subtract the one-time fee.
m = 8Divide by 15. After 8 months.

Notice: the one-time fee is a plain number and the repeating charge is attached to the variable. Nearly every "fee plus rate" problem has that shape — and so does y = mx + b, which you'll meet in Unit 3.

When two things are related, define one variable

“Alex has twice as many stickers as Sam.” Don't use two letters. Let s = Sam's stickers; then Alex has 2s. One unknown, one equation.

Pick the smaller quantity as your variable — the one the other is described in terms of. It keeps the numbers positive and the algebra simple.

Write it like the rubric is watching

Word problems are where the syllabus's "communicate mathematical reasoning clearly" actually gets graded. A bare number at the bottom of the page loses marks even when it's right. Four lines get you full credit:

  • Define the variable. "Let w = the width in cm." Not just "w" — say what it is and its units.
  • Write the equation. On its own line.
  • Solve it, showing the steps.
  • Answer the question in a sentence, with units: "The width is 9 cm." The question asked for a width, not for w = 9.

That last line also catches your own errors: if the sentence sounds absurd — a negative width, 2.5 people — you know to go back.

Ask your tutor. If a problem still doesn't make sense after you read the solution, say so — you'll get it explained a different way. Stuck on an assigned problem? Don't paste it in; ask for a similar one with different numbers.
